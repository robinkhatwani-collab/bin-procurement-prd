Topic 1 of 18
What is Fine-Tuning?

Fine-tuning is the process of taking a pre-trained foundation model and continuing its training on a smaller, task-specific dataset — so the model specialises in your domain, tone, format, or task without being built from scratch.

Think of it as the difference between hiring a general consultant (foundation model) and a consultant who's spent 3 months embedded in your company (fine-tuned model). They already know how to think — now they know your business.

🧠 What Fine-Tuning Changes
Fine-tuning updates the model's weights — the internal numerical parameters that determine how it processes language. This makes the change permanent and baked in, unlike a system prompt which is applied at runtime and can be overridden.
✅ What Fine-Tuning Is Good For
Domain-specific vocabulary and writing style · Consistent output format (always respond as JSON) · Task specialisation (always classify, never explain) · Reducing latency by compressing instructions into weights · Reducing cost by using a smaller base model with specialist training
❌ What Fine-Tuning Cannot Do
Add new factual knowledge (it won't learn your real-time data) · Replace retrieval for current or dynamic information · Guarantee accuracy on facts it wasn't trained on · Make a small model perform like a large one on complex reasoning
📋 PM Tip — Fine-Tuning is Expensive to Maintain
Every time the base model provider releases a new version, you may need to re-fine-tune. Build this maintenance cost into your roadmap before committing to fine-tuning as your primary strategy.

Fine-Tuning vs RAG vs Prompt Engineering — Decision Framework

These three approaches are often confused. They solve different problems and are frequently used together. Here's how to decide which to reach for first.

🧭 The Decision Ladder
Start with prompt engineering. If it solves the problem, ship it.
Add RAG if the model needs access to current, proprietary, or large-scale facts.
Fine-tune only when you need consistent style/format, domain vocabulary, or you've squeezed everything from the above two and quality still falls short.

Topic 3 of 18
Types of Fine-Tuning

There is no single fine-tuning method. The approach you choose depends on your budget, data volume, and how much you want to change the model's behaviour.

Full Fine-Tuning
All model weights are updated on your dataset. Maximum control and performance gain — but requires significant compute, large labelled datasets (10k+ examples), and a full retraining cycle for every model update.
📍 Use when: you have significant data, budget, and need maximum specialisation.
LoRA / QLoRA (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning)
Only a small set of adapter parameters are trained — the base model weights are frozen. Dramatically cheaper (can run on a single GPU), faster to iterate, and easy to swap adapters. LoRA is the dominant fine-tuning method for most teams in 2024–25.
📍 Use when: you want to fine-tune cheaply on consumer or cloud GPU. Best starting point for most teams.
Instruction Tuning
Fine-tune the model to follow instructions reliably — using datasets of (instruction, response) pairs. This is how base models become "chat" models. Use it to teach a model a domain-specific task format.
📍 Use when: you need the model to reliably follow a specific task structure (e.g., always extract 5 fields from a document in JSON).
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)
Human raters score model outputs, and a reward model is trained on those scores. The LLM is then fine-tuned to maximise reward. This is how OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google align their frontier models. Expensive, complex, and rarely done at the product team level.
📍 Use when: you're building a foundation model or need deep alignment adjustment. Not a typical PM-level decision.
RLAIF / DPO (Newer Alternatives to RLHF)
Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO) skips the reward model entirely, training directly from preference pairs (preferred response A vs rejected response B). Simpler and increasingly popular for product teams doing preference-based alignment.
📍 Use when: you want RLHF-quality alignment without the complexity. Growing fast in 2025.

What are Embeddings?

An embedding is a numerical representation of text (or an image, audio clip, etc.) as a point in high-dimensional space. Words, sentences, or documents that are semantically similar end up close together in that space — even if they use different words.

💡 The Geography Analogy
Imagine a map where every concept has a location. "Fraud" and "unauthorised transaction" end up near each other on the map, even though the words are different. "Groceries" and "dining" cluster together. "Credit limit" sits near "spending cap." Embeddings are that map — just in 1,536 dimensions instead of 2.

Embeddings power three critical AI capabilities:

🔍
Semantic Search
Find documents by meaning, not keyword. "When does my statement close?" matches "billing cycle end date."
🗂️
RAG Retrieval
The retrieval step in RAG converts the user query into an embedding and fetches the nearest document chunks.
🏷️
Classification
Train a classifier on top of embeddings to categorise transactions, tickets, or feedback without a large labelled dataset.
🧩
Deduplication
Find near-duplicate disputes, similar merchant names, or repeated customer complaints by proximity in embedding space.
📋 PM Tip — Embedding Models vs LLMs
Embedding models (text-embedding-3-large, Cohere Embed) are separate from chat LLMs. They're cheaper, faster, and purpose-built for similarity. Don't use an LLM to compare documents — use an embedding model.

Vector Databases

A vector database is purpose-built to store, index, and query embeddings at scale. It answers the question: "Which stored vectors are closest to this query vector?" — in milliseconds, across millions of documents.

DatabaseBest ForKey StrengthConsideration
PineconeProduction SaaS, fast setupFully managed, low ops overheadVendor lock-in, cost at scale
WeaviateOpen-source + hybrid searchCombines vector + keyword searchMore ops complexity
pgvectorTeams already on PostgreSQLNo new infrastructure neededSlower at very large scale
ChromaPrototyping & local devLightweight, great for testingNot production-grade at scale
QdrantHigh-performance self-hostedFast, Rust-based, payload filteringNewer, smaller community
🏗️ How It Fits Into a RAG Pipeline
Ingest: chunk documents → embed each chunk → store vectors in the DB
Query: embed the user question → search DB for nearest chunks → inject chunks into LLM prompt → generate grounded answer

Semantic Search vs Keyword Search

Understanding this distinction is essential for any PM building a search or retrieval feature. They find documents in fundamentally different ways.

How it worksMatches exact or stemmed wordsMatches by meaning and context

✅ Hybrid Search — Best of Both
Most production search systems use hybrid search — run both keyword and semantic retrieval, then re-rank the merged results. This handles both "BIN 424242" (keyword) and "why was my payment declined" (semantic) in the same pipeline.

Data Strategy & Data Flywheels

For AI products, data is the moat. The model is a commodity — anyone can API-call GPT-4o. The proprietary dataset you accumulate is what competitors can't copy.

🔄 The Data Flywheel
More users → more interactions → more labelled data → better model → better product → more users.

The key PM insight: every user interaction is a data asset. Design your product to capture implicit feedback (did the user accept the AI suggestion? Did they edit it? Did they re-ask?) — not just explicit ratings.

Five data strategy decisions every AI PM must make:

  • Collection: What signals will you capture? Clicks, edits, thumbs up/down, task completion?
  • Labelling: Who labels the data? Internal SMEs, outsourced annotators, or synthetic generation?
  • Quality: How do you measure and maintain annotation quality? Inter-annotator agreement?
  • Governance: Can this data be used for training? Check user consent and T&Cs.
  • Freshness: How quickly does data go stale? How often do you need to refresh training data?
📋 PM Tip — Synthetic Data
When you lack real training data, use the LLM itself to generate labelled examples — a technique called synthetic data generation. Works well for edge cases, rare events (like unusual fraud patterns), and bootstrapping a new model before real data arrives.

Topic 8 of 18
Model Monitoring & Observability

AI models degrade silently. Unlike a software bug that crashes visibly, a drifting model just gets quietly worse. Monitoring is what catches this before users do.

There are two types of drift to watch for:

📊 Data Drift
The distribution of real inputs changes vs what the model was trained on. Example: a fraud model trained pre-2023 never saw "AI-generated phishing emails" as a fraud vector.
📉 Concept Drift
The relationship between input and correct output changes. Example: "contactless payment" used to be unusual (flag it). Now it's normal (don't flag it).
Metric to MonitorWhat It CatchesAlert Threshold Example
Prediction distributionModel outputting unusually high/low fraud scoresDaily fraud rate >3σ from 30-day baseline
Output confidenceModel becoming uncertain across all inputsMean confidence drops >10% week-over-week
Latency P95Inference slowdowns affecting user experienceP95 latency >500ms for 5 min
Hallucination rateLLM quality regression after model updateWeekly eval score drops >5%
User override rateUsers rejecting AI suggestions more often>20% of suggestions edited or dismissed
📋 PM Tip — Shadow Mode Deployment
Before fully switching to a new model version, run it in shadow mode — it processes real traffic and logs outputs, but doesn't serve users. Compare shadow outputs to the live model for 1–2 weeks before cutting over. Prevents silent regressions.

Topic 9 of 18
AI Incident Management

When an AI model fails in production — whether by hallucinating, producing biased outputs, or being exploited — you need a clear runbook. AI incidents are different from software incidents because the cause is often probabilistic and hard to reproduce exactly.

Severity 1 — Model producing harmful or discriminatory outputs at scale
Immediate action: Kill switch — revert to the previous model version or disable the AI feature entirely. Notify your safety and legal teams within the hour. Do not attempt to patch the prompt in production.
Severity 2 — Significant quality regression noticed by users
Action: Run your eval suite immediately to quantify the regression. If score drops >10%, roll back. Flag to engineering and model team. Communicate to affected users if decisions were impacted.
Severity 3 — Minor quality issue or edge case failure
Action: Log the failing examples. Add them to your eval suite. Schedule a prompt or fine-tuning fix in the next sprint. No emergency action required.
✅ The AI Incident Checklist
  • Do you have a kill switch that can disable the AI feature in <5 minutes?
  • Is there a human fallback path for every AI decision that affects users?
  • Is there a communication template ready for user-impacting AI failures?
  • Does your post-mortem process cover model behaviour, not just system uptime?

Writing AI PRDs — What's Different

An AI PRD is structurally similar to a standard PRD, but it must answer several questions that never appear in traditional software specs. Skipping these is how AI features ship without a safety plan.

Success MetricsDAU, conversion, NPS+ Accuracy, hallucination rate, human override rate, latency P95
Failure ModesOften absentRequired: worst-case output, mitigation, kill switch
Data RequirementsN/ATraining data, eval dataset, labelling process, data consent
Human OversightN/AWhen does a human review? What can the model decide autonomously?
ExplainabilityN/ACan users understand why the AI decided X? Is that legally required?

📋 The One Sentence Test
Every AI PRD should be able to answer: "What is the worst thing this model could plausibly do, and what is our plan if it happens?" If your PRD can't answer this, it's not ready to ship.

Topic 11 of 18
AI Feature Discovery — Finding Where AI Adds Value

Not every problem needs AI. The PM's job is to identify where AI creates disproportionate value — where it does something that rule-based systems, humans, or traditional software cannot match.

AI adds the most value when tasks share these properties:

High volume, low unit value — too many decisions for humans to make manually (categorise 50M transactions, triage 100k support tickets).
Pattern recognition in unstructured data — extracting meaning from text, images, audio that rules can't handle.
Personalisation at scale — different outputs for every user, based on context and history.
Natural language interaction — where the interface benefit of free-text input outweighs the cost of handling variability.
Augmenting expert judgement — helping a specialist (fraud analyst, compliance officer) work faster without replacing their decision authority.
📋 PM Tip — The 3-Question Filter
Before pursuing any AI feature: (1) Could a well-written rule or formula solve this adequately? (2) Do we have (or can we get) the data to make this work? (3) Is the cost of being wrong acceptable? If you can't answer all three, pause.

A/B Testing AI Features — Unique Challenges

A/B testing AI features follows the same statistical principles as traditional experiments — but introduces several complications that standard testing frameworks don't handle well.

ChallengeWhy It's Different for AIHow to Handle It
Non-determinismSame prompt → different output each run. Hard to reproduce "variant A."Fix temperature=0 for controlled tests; log inputs and outputs for audit.
Metrics lagAI quality impact (e.g. trust erosion) shows up weeks after exposure.Run longer experiments (4+ weeks); track downstream metrics, not just click-through.
Novelty effectUsers engage more with AI just because it's new, not because it's better.Run a holdout group for 6+ weeks; measure sustained engagement, not first-week spikes.
ContaminationAI recommendations influence user behaviour, which affects the control group too.Use cluster randomisation (by user or account) rather than request-level splits.
Safety can't be A/B testedYou can't show harmful outputs to 50% of users to "test" the impact.Use offline red team evals to assess safety before any live experiment.
✅ What to Measure in an AI A/B Test
Task success rate — did the user accomplish their goal?
Override/edit rate — how often do users reject or change the AI output?
Time-to-completion — is the AI making users faster?
Escalation rate — are users needing human help less often?
Return rate — do users keep using the feature after first try?

Topic 13 of 18
User Research for AI Products

Standard user research methods still apply to AI products — but several new questions emerge that traditional UX interviews don't cover.

🆕 New Questions to Ask
How much do they trust AI outputs? When do they verify? What makes them distrust? Have they been burned by AI before? Do they feel the AI "understands" their intent?
🔍 New Research Methods
Think-aloud testing with AI outputs. Wizard of Oz (human pretending to be AI). Longitudinal studies to measure trust calibration over time.

Three trust anti-patterns to test for:

  • Over-trust: Users accept all AI outputs without checking — even clearly wrong ones. Risk: errors propagate.
  • Under-trust: Users ignore AI output and do everything manually. Risk: AI adds no value.
  • Miscalibrated trust: Users trust AI on the tasks where it's weakest, and distrust it on the tasks where it's strongest. Most common and hardest to fix.

Build vs Buy vs Fine-Tune vs Prompt — Decision Framework

Every AI product decision starts here. Getting this wrong is the most expensive mistake a PM can make — either over-engineering (building what you could buy) or under-investing (prompting where you needed fine-tuning).

Just Prompt It
Use an off-the-shelf model API with a well-crafted system prompt. No training, no infra. Ship in days.
✅ Use when: the task is general, quality is acceptable, and you're iterating fast. Start here by default.
Add RAG
Keep the off-the-shelf model, add a retrieval layer over your proprietary documents or real-time data.
✅ Use when: the model needs facts it wasn't trained on, data changes frequently, or you need citations.
Buy / Integrate a Specialist Model
Use a third-party model purpose-built for your domain (e.g. a fraud-specific model, a medical NLP API).
✅ Use when: a specialist provider exists, their accuracy beats general models, and build cost exceeds buy cost over 2 years.
Fine-Tune an Existing Model
Take a foundation model and train it on your domain data. Requires data, compute, and ongoing maintenance.
✅ Use when: general models consistently underperform on your task, you have 1k+ labelled examples, and latency/cost savings justify the investment.
Build From Scratch
Train your own foundation model. Only viable for very large organisations with unique data, massive compute budgets, and dedicated ML research teams.
✅ Use when: you are OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, or Meta. Almost never the right call for a product team.

AI Business Models & Competitive Moats

The model is not the moat — it's a commodity. Sustainable AI businesses are built on what wraps the model: proprietary data, workflow integration, switching costs, and network effects.

Moat TypeHow AI Creates ItCredit Card Example
Data FlywheelMore usage → more labelled data → better model → more usageEvery fraud decision teaches the model; competitors without that history can't match accuracy
Workflow Lock-inAI deeply embedded in daily workflows is hard to rip outAnalysts trained on AI-assisted dispute triage won't switch to a manual tool
Network EffectsAI improves as more users join the same modelConsortium fraud models improve for all members as the network grows
Proprietary DataUnique training data competitors can't accessDecades of anonymised transaction history is impossible to replicate
API-First DistributionBecome the infrastructure others build onSelling fraud scoring as an API to other issuers
⚠️ The Thin Wrapper Trap
A product that simply wraps an LLM API with a nice UI has no moat. Any competitor can replicate the wrapper in a weekend. The question every AI PM must ask: "If OpenAI ships this feature natively tomorrow, does our product survive?" If the answer is no, you need to build a deeper moat.

AI ROI & Building the Business Case

AI projects frequently get approved based on vague promises of efficiency. A rigorous business case forces clarity on costs, benefits, and the measurement plan — before a single line of code is written.

The AI ROI Formula:

ROI = (Value Created − Total Cost of Ownership) / Total Cost of Ownership

Value Created = cost savings (hours saved × headcount cost) + revenue uplift (higher conversion, fewer chargebacks) + risk reduction (regulatory fines avoided)

Total Cost of Ownership = model API costs + engineering build + data labelling + ongoing evals + compliance + monitoring + model refresh cycles
📋 PM Tip — Most Teams Underestimate TCO by 3×
The API call cost is visible. The hidden costs — annotation, evals, monitoring, red teaming, model updates, compliance review — are not. Build a 3-year TCO model before presenting an AI business case. The first year almost never pays off.

Three ROI archetypes in credit card AI:

  • Cost reduction: AI handles Tier-1 disputes → 40% reduction in contact centre volume → $X million saved annually
  • Revenue protection: Better fraud detection → fewer false positives → fewer legitimate transactions blocked → $X million in preserved revenue
  • Risk reduction: AI-assisted compliance review → faster regulatory submissions → fewer fines → $X million in avoided penalties

Go-to-Market Strategy for AI Features

Launching an AI feature requires a different GTM playbook than traditional software — primarily because user trust must be built deliberately, and failure modes are visible and reputational.

1. Staged Rollout (not Big Bang)
Start with 1–5% of users. Monitor error rates, hallucinations, and user override rates. Expand only when each cohort passes your quality gates. AI failures at 100% rollout are reputational catastrophes.
2. Set Expectations Early
Label AI features clearly ("Suggested by AI"). Disclose confidence levels where appropriate. Users who know they're using AI calibrate trust better and are more forgiving of errors.
3. Human Fallback Always Visible
Never remove the non-AI path entirely at launch. Users who distrust the AI feature need an out. Removing it triggers churn and regulatory scrutiny.
4. Internal Champions First
Before external launch, deploy to internal teams (customer service, fraud ops, compliance). They find failure modes faster than any QA process and build the human feedback loop you need for the data flywheel.
5. Build the Narrative, Not Just the Feature
AI features need a clear "why" for both users and regulators. "Our AI reduces dispute resolution time from 10 days to 48 hours" is a story. "AI-powered disputes" is not. Define the before/after and make it measurable.

Topic 18 of 18
Week 6 Key Takeaways

Applied AI PM is about connecting the technical and the commercial — knowing when to fine-tune vs prompt, how to monitor what you ship, and how to build a business case that survives scrutiny.

01
Start with prompting, always. The decision ladder is: prompt → RAG → buy → fine-tune → build. Most problems are solved before you reach fine-tuning.
02
LoRA is the practical fine-tuning default. Full fine-tuning is expensive and brittle. LoRA gives you 80% of the benefit at 10% of the cost — it's where most teams should start.
03
Embeddings power more than search. Classification, deduplication, anomaly detection, and RAG retrieval all run on embeddings. Understanding them unlocks half the AI toolbox.
04
AI models degrade silently. Monitoring is not optional — it's the difference between catching drift in your dashboard and catching it in a headline. Build monitoring before launch, not after.
05
An AI PRD must answer the worst-case question. If your spec doesn't describe the failure mode and the mitigation, it's not ready to ship.
06
Data is the real moat. The model is a commodity. The proprietary data flywheel you build through user interactions is what competitors cannot replicate.
07
TCO is 3× what you see. API costs are the tip of the iceberg. Labelling, evals, monitoring, compliance, and model refresh cycles are what sink AI business cases that looked profitable on a spreadsheet.
08
Launch staged, build trust deliberately. AI GTM is about earning trust incrementally — with users, with regulators, and with your own organisation. Big bang AI launches almost always end badly.
