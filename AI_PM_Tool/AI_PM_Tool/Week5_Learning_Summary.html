What is AI Safety?

AI Safety is the field focused on ensuring AI systems behave as intended, don't cause unintended harm, and remain aligned with human values — both now and as systems become more capable.

For a Product Manager, AI safety isn't just a technical concern. It's a product quality dimension. An unsafe AI feature ships with hidden defects that can harm users, create legal liability, and destroy trust in your brand — often in ways that are much harder to reverse than a typical software bug.

🧭 The Two Dimensions of AI Safety
Capability safety — the model can do what it claims (accuracy, reliability, robustness).
Value alignment — the model behaves within ethical boundaries (fairness, transparency, no harmful outputs).

Both matter. A highly capable but misaligned model is dangerous. A perfectly aligned but incapable model is useless.

AI safety failures range from minor (wrong product recommendation) to severe (discriminatory loan decisions, medical misdiagnosis, generating harmful content). The PM's job is to understand where their product sits on that spectrum and design safeguards accordingly.

Bias & Fairness

AI bias occurs when a model systematically produces outputs that are unfair toward certain groups. It almost always originates from the training data — if the data reflects historical inequalities, the model learns and amplifies them.

📊 Historical Bias
Training data reflects past inequalities. Example: a hiring model trained on 10 years of hires at a male-dominated company learns that "engineer = male."
🔍 Representation Bias
Certain groups are underrepresented in training data. The model performs well for majority groups and poorly for minorities.
🏷️ Label Bias
Human annotators introduced their own biases when labelling training data. The model inherits those subjective judgements.
📐 Measurement Bias
The proxy metric used for training doesn't equally measure what it claims across groups. Example: using "past arrest records" as a proxy for recidivism penalises over-policed communities.
✅ Fairness Mitigations for PMs
Audit your training data — check demographic distributions before training.
Test across subgroups — disaggregate accuracy metrics by gender, age, ethnicity.
Set fairness KPIs — e.g. "false positive rate must not differ by more than 5% across groups."
Use diverse annotation teams — reduces label bias at the source.

Hallucinations — Why LLMs Confabulate

Hallucination is when an LLM generates text that is confident, fluent, and completely wrong. The model isn't "lying" — it's pattern-matching on language without grounding in factual truth. It produces whatever token sequence is statistically likely, not whatever is accurate.

💡 Why It Happens
LLMs are trained to predict the next token, not to verify facts. During generation, the model maximises linguistic coherence — a plausible-sounding fabricated answer can score higher in this process than an honest "I don't know."

Hallucinations are most common when:

  • The question is about niche, recent, or very specific facts
  • The model is asked to recall numbers, dates, or citations
  • The system prompt provides no retrieval context (no RAG)
  • The model is pushed to answer outside its knowledge cutoff
MitigationHow It HelpsTradeoff
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) Grounds responses in retrieved source documents Adds latency; retrieval quality matters
Citation enforcement Prompt forces model to cite its source inline Model can still fabricate citations
Confidence thresholding Surface uncertainty scores to users LLMs are often overconfident
Human-in-the-loop Expert reviews before output is shown Adds cost and latency
Knowledge cutoff disclosure Tells users what the model doesn't know Users may still over-trust
📋 PM Tip — Hallucination Budget
Define an acceptable hallucination rate before launch. A customer support bot might tolerate 1%; a medical advice tool should tolerate 0%. Set this as a measurable launch gate criterion.

Explainability & Interpretability (XAI)

Explainability (XAI) is the ability to describe — in human-understandable terms — why an AI system produced a particular output. It answers: "Why did the model decide this?"

There's an important distinction between two related terms:

🔭
Interpretability
Understanding the model's internal mechanics — what features, weights, or representations drive decisions. Requires ML expertise.
💬
Explainability
Providing a post-hoc justification a user can understand — "this loan was denied because your debt-to-income ratio exceeded 43%."

Common XAI techniques:

  • SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) — shows each feature's contribution to a prediction
  • LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) — explains individual predictions using a simpler local model
  • Attention visualisation — shows which tokens the model focused on (limited reliability for LLMs)
  • Chain-of-thought prompting — asks the model to reason step-by-step, surfacing its logic
📋 PM Tip — When XAI is Required
Regulated industries (lending, insurance, hiring, healthcare) often legally require an explanation for automated decisions affecting individuals. Build XAI into the product spec, not as an afterthought.

Red Teaming AI Systems

Red teaming is a structured adversarial testing process where a dedicated team deliberately tries to break, mislead, or elicit harmful behaviour from your AI system — before real users do.

⚔️ What Red Teams Look For
Prompt injection — can a malicious user override system instructions?
Jailbreaks — can the model be tricked into ignoring safety constraints?
Data extraction — can the model be made to reveal confidential training data or system prompts?
Bias amplification — do adversarial inputs surface discriminatory behaviour?
Sycophancy — does the model agree with false premises to please the user?

Red teaming is not just for launch — it should be continuous. As models are updated, fine-tuned, or connected to new tools, new attack surfaces emerge.

📋 PM Tip — Red Team as a Launch Gate
Make a red team sign-off a mandatory step in your launch checklist, alongside performance testing and accessibility review. Define a minimum pass bar (e.g. "no critical jailbreaks found") before releasing to production.

GDPR & CCPA — Data Privacy Laws

Two major privacy regulations shape what AI products can legally do with user data. Both impose obligations on how data is collected, stored, used, and explained to users.

DimensionGDPR (EU)CCPA (California)
Scope Any company processing EU residents' data California residents; businesses above revenue/data thresholds
Lawful basis Consent, contract, legitimate interest (must document) Opt-out model — users can opt out of sale of data
Right to explanation Article 22: right to explanation for automated decisions No equivalent right (yet)
Data minimisation Required — collect only what you need Not explicitly required
Penalties Up to 4% of global annual revenue Up to $7,500 per intentional violation
📋 PM Tip — AI and GDPR Article 22
If your AI makes automated decisions that significantly affect users (credit, employment, healthcare), GDPR gives users the right to request human review. Design your product so this pathway exists — it must be accessible, not buried in a support ticket form.

Topic 8 of 14
EU AI Act — The World's First AI Law

The EU AI Act (effective 2024–2026) is the world's first comprehensive legal framework for AI. It classifies AI systems by risk level and imposes proportionate requirements on each tier.

🚫 Unacceptable Risk — BANNED
Social scoring by governments, real-time remote biometric surveillance in public spaces, manipulation of vulnerable groups. These are prohibited outright.
⚠️ High Risk — REGULATED
AI in credit, employment, education, healthcare, and critical infrastructure. Must meet transparency, data quality, human oversight, and accuracy requirements before deployment.
💬 Limited Risk — TRANSPARENCY OBLIGATIONS
Chatbots and AI-generated content must disclose they are AI. Users must always know they're interacting with a machine.
✅ Minimal Risk — FREE TO USE
AI-powered spam filters, video games, recommendation engines. No specific legal requirements, but voluntary codes of conduct encouraged.
📋 PM Tip — Know Your Risk Tier
Before building, classify your product on the EU AI Act risk ladder. High-risk products need a conformity assessment, technical documentation, and a human oversight plan before you can ship in the EU. Build compliance into your roadmap, not as a retrofit.

AI Risk Categories — A PM's Taxonomy

Beyond legal frameworks, PMs benefit from a practical taxonomy of risks to assess new features quickly. Use this as a mental checklist when scoping any AI-powered capability.

CategoryKey Questions to Ask
Output Risk What is the worst thing this model could plausibly say or do? Who is harmed if it happens?
Data Risk Does training or inference use personal data? Could outputs reveal that data?
Dependency Risk What happens if the underlying model API goes down, changes its behaviour, or is deprecated?
Feedback Loop Risk Could the model's outputs influence the data it is later trained on, creating a runaway loop?
Autonomy Risk Can the model take irreversible real-world actions (send emails, make payments, delete records)?
Concentration Risk Are we over-relying on a single model provider? What is the exit strategy?

PM Safety Checklist — Before You Ship

Use this checklist as a launch gate for any AI-powered feature. It won't catch every risk, but it ensures the most common ones have been considered.

  • Defined the worst-case output scenario and documented a mitigation for it
  • Audited training and fine-tuning data for demographic representation gaps
  • Disaggregated evaluation metrics by key subgroups (gender, age, region)
  • Ran red team exercises and documented findings; critical issues resolved
  • Set a measurable hallucination rate threshold and verified it is met
  • Identified which EU AI Act risk tier this product falls into
  • Confirmed GDPR / CCPA compliance for all personal data in the pipeline
  • Added user-facing disclosure that the feature is AI-powered
  • Designed a human override / escalation path for consequential decisions
  • Established a model monitoring plan with defined drift alert thresholds
  • Documented model version, training cutoff, and known limitations for the support team
  • Assigned an accountable owner for ongoing responsible AI review

AI Leaderboards & Agent Benchmarks

An AI leaderboard is a public ranking table that scores and compares AI models on standardised benchmark tasks. They give PMs and engineers an objective, third-party view of how models compare — before spending a dollar on API calls.

🏅 The Most Referenced Leaderboards
Chatbot Arena (LMSYS) — human-preference leaderboard. Real users compare two models side-by-side and vote; results are statistically ranked. Closest proxy to real user satisfaction.

Open LLM Leaderboard (Hugging Face) — automated benchmark scores across reasoning, coding, and knowledge tasks for open-source models.

HELM (Stanford) — Holistic Evaluation of Language Models. Measures 42+ metrics including accuracy, calibration, fairness, and efficiency across 30+ scenarios.

BIG-Bench — 204 diverse tasks designed to be hard for current models; tests reasoning, creativity, and common sense at scale.

For AI agents specifically, standard chat benchmarks aren't enough — agents need to be evaluated on multi-step task completion. Key agent benchmarks include:

BenchmarkWhat It TestsWhy It Matters
SWE-bench Can the agent resolve real GitHub issues in actual codebases? Gold standard for software engineering agents
WebArena Can the agent complete tasks on real websites (shopping, email, maps)? Measures real-world browser autonomy
AgentBench 8 distinct environments — OS, DB, web browsing, card games, and more Broadest multi-environment agent eval
GAIA General AI Assistants — real-world questions needing tools, reasoning, and search Tests end-to-end agentic reasoning chains
τ-bench (Tau-bench) Agent handles realistic customer service conversations with tool use Best proxy for production support agents
📋 PM Tip — Leaderboards Are a Starting Point, Not a Decision
Leaderboard scores are averages over generic tasks. Your product has specific tasks. Always run your own eval on a representative sample of your real workload — a model that ranks #3 overall might rank #1 for your specific use case.

Topic 12 of 14
MLCommons — What It Is & How It Operates

MLCommons is a non-profit industry consortium that builds open, standardised benchmarks and datasets for the AI industry. Its goal is to make AI measurement reproducible, fair, and independent of any single company's interests.

Think of MLCommons as the "ISO standards body" for AI evaluation — it sets shared measurement rules so the industry can compare apples to apples.

⚡ MLPerf (Performance)
The flagship benchmark suite measuring how fast hardware and software systems can train and run AI models. Covers training speed, inference throughput, and energy efficiency. Used by NVIDIA, Google, Intel, and others to prove hardware claims.
🛡️ AI Safety Working Group
Develops standardised safety evaluation frameworks so the industry can measure harm, bias, and reliability using the same ruler. Feeds into regulatory discussions like the EU AI Act's conformity assessments.
📚 Open Datasets
MLCommons releases high-quality open datasets for training and evaluation — including the People's Speech dataset (the world's largest open English speech corpus) and medical imaging datasets.
🤝 How It Operates
Member organisations (including Google, Meta, NVIDIA, Arm, and Hugging Face) contribute engineering time and resources. Benchmarks are developed openly, run under strict rules, and results are published publicly. No member can modify the benchmark rules to favour their own product.
📋 PM Tip — Why MLCommons Matters to You
When a vendor says "our model is 40% faster," ask: faster on what benchmark? If it's not an MLPerf result, it may be cherry-picked. MLCommons benchmarks are the industry's shared language for hardware and model performance claims.

How to Pick the Right Model Using Benchmarks

Choosing a model isn't just about "which one scores highest." It's a product decision that balances capability, cost, latency, safety, and strategic risk. Benchmarks give you signal — but you need a framework to translate that signal into a decision.

Step 1 — Define your task type first. Benchmarks are only relevant if they match your workload:

Your Use CaseMost Relevant Benchmarks
General reasoning / QA MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC, TruthfulQA
Code generation HumanEval, MBPP, SWE-bench
Long document understanding SCROLLS, ZeroScrolls, RULER (long-context)
Agentic / tool use AgentBench, GAIA, τ-bench, WebArena
Multilingual FLORES, XTREME, Multilingual MMLU
Safety / harmful output ToxiGen, BBQ (bias), HarmBench
User satisfaction (chat) Chatbot Arena Elo score

Step 2 — Apply the PM Model Selection Framework. Score candidate models across five dimensions:

🎯
Task Fit
Benchmark score on tasks that match your specific workload. Not overall rank.
💰
Cost / Token
Input + output token pricing. A 10× cheaper model at 90% quality often wins.
⚡
Latency
Time-to-first-token and total generation time. Critical for real-time UX.
🛡️
Safety Score
Refusal rate, toxicity rate, hallucination rate on your domain.
🔒
Strategic Risk
Vendor lock-in, data privacy terms, API stability, exit strategy.
🧭 The Golden Rule of Model Selection
Never select a model based on benchmarks alone. Run a custom eval on 100–200 representative examples from your real product. The delta between benchmark rank and real-world rank can be huge — especially for domain-specific tasks. Benchmarks shortlist; your eval decides.
📋 PM Tip — Model Selection is an Ongoing Decision
The model landscape changes every 6–8 weeks. Build a lightweight model eval pipeline you can re-run automatically when a new model releases. The best model today may not be the best model at launch — and definitely won't be the best model in 12 months.
💳 Credit Card Domain — Model Selection Case Studies
Fraud Real-time Transaction Fraud Scoring

Scenario: A card network needs to score every transaction for fraud risk in under 50ms at peak volume of 5,000 transactions/second. The model must explain its decision to the fraud ops team when a card is declined.

Why a generic LLM fails here: LLMs are too slow (200–800ms) and too expensive at transaction scale. This is a structured prediction problem, not a language task.

Model TypeLatencyCost/1M callsExplainabilityVerdict
GPT-4o~600ms~$30Chain-of-thoughtSkip
Fine-tuned BERT<10ms~$0.20Attention mapsAlert triage only
XGBoost + SHAP<5ms~$0.05SHAP valuesPick for scoring
Claude Haiku (async)~300ms~$1.00Natural languagePick for explanation

Winning pattern: A hybrid architecture — XGBoost scores in real-time (<5ms); when declined, Claude Haiku asynchronously generates a plain-English explanation for the fraud analyst. Best of both worlds.

CX Dispute Resolution Chatbot

Scenario: A card issuer wants to automate Tier-1 dispute handling — where cardholders report unrecognised charges. The bot must gather evidence, explain the process, and decide if the dispute qualifies for auto-provisional credit.

Key requirements: Multi-turn conversation, retrieval of transaction history, ability to follow Regulation E / chargeback rules, and a warm handoff to a human agent when the case is complex.

Risk Credit Limit Recommendation Engine

Scenario: The risk team wants an AI system to recommend personalised credit limit increases for eligible customers, with a natural-language rationale the customer can read in the app.

Two distinct sub-tasks: (1) predicting the optimal limit from structured data, (2) generating a human-readable explanation of why the limit changed.

Sub-taskBest Model TypeWhy
Limit predictionGradient Boosting (LightGBM)Tabular data, low latency, SHAP-explainable, easy to audit for bias
Explanation generationClaude Haiku or GPT-4o miniLow cost, fast, high-quality short-form text, tone control via system prompt
Bias auditAequitas / FairlearnNot an LLM — dedicated fairness tooling required for ECOA compliance

Regulatory note: ECOA (Equal Credit Opportunity Act) requires that adverse action notices state specific reasons. The LLM-generated explanation must be grounded in the model's actual SHAP output — not hallucinated reasons.

Ops Transaction Categorisation at Scale

Scenario: A fintech needs to categorise 50 million transactions/month into 40 merchant categories (groceries, travel, dining, etc.) to power spend analytics in its mobile app.

Challenge: Merchant names are messy ("AMZN MKTP US*1F3K2", "SQ *BLUE BOTTLE") — rule-based systems hit a 15% error rate on long-tail merchants.

ApproachAccuracyCost/1M txnsLatencyVerdict

Winning pattern: Tiered routing — fine-tuned DistilBERT handles 95% of transactions in batch; GPT-4o mini handles the <5% low-confidence cases. Cost drops from $900 → ~$14 per million while maintaining 97%+ accuracy.

Compliance Contract & Policy Document Review

Scenario: The legal team at a card issuer needs to review incoming merchant agreement amendments and flag clauses that deviate from standard terms (interchange rates, liability caps, data-use restrictions).

Key requirements: Long-context reasoning (contracts can be 40–80 pages), precise clause extraction, citation of the exact paragraph, low hallucination tolerance.

Week 5 Key Takeaways

Responsible AI is not a compliance checkbox — it's the quality bar for AI products. These are the ideas from this week that every AI PM should carry forward.

01
Safety is a product quality dimension. An unsafe AI feature ships with hidden defects — often irreversible ones. Define your safety requirements before writing a line of code.
02
Bias lives in data. If your training data reflects historical inequalities, your model will amplify them. Audit data distributions before training, not after complaints.
03
Hallucinations are a design problem. RAG, citation enforcement, and human review can reduce them — but you must define an acceptable rate and measure it continuously.
04
Know your EU AI Act tier. High-risk AI requires documentation, oversight design, and conformity assessment before you can legally deploy in Europe. Build compliance in, not on.
05
Red teaming is a launch gate. Assume users will try to break your system. A dedicated red team exercise before launch catches exploits that testing never will.
06
Leaderboards shortlist — your eval decides. Chatbot Arena and HELM give you an objective starting point, but only a custom eval on your real workload can tell you which model actually fits your product.
07
MLCommons is the ISO for AI benchmarks. When a vendor quotes performance numbers, ask whether they're MLPerf results. Proprietary benchmarks are almost always cherry-picked in the vendor's favour.
08
Model selection has five dimensions. Task fit, cost per token, latency, safety score, and strategic risk. Optimising only on capability will lead to a model that's too expensive, too slow, or too locked-in for production.
